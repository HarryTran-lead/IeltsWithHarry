# ESL-L-04 | Original Listening Test 04

## Part 1 | Regional Rail Booking
intro: a conversation between a traveller and a clerk at a railway station
voices: Clerk=Orus, Traveller=Aoede

### 1-6
Clerk: Good morning. How can I help you?
Traveller: Hello. I'd like some information about trains to the coast this Saturday, please. I'm going to visit my grandmother, and I haven't travelled by train for ages.
Clerk: Of course. Whereabouts on the coast does she live?
Traveller: Just outside Westhaven. I know there isn't a station in her village, so I'd go to Westhaven and she'd pick me up from there.
Clerk: That's easy, then. There are two ways of getting there. You can take the stopping train and change at Bramley, or there's a faster option. The direct service runs to Westhaven. It only runs on Saturdays and Sundays, but it saves you about forty minutes.
Traveller: The direct one sounds perfect. What time does it go?
Clerk: There are two in the morning. The first one's quite early. The train leaves at 7:25. After that, the next one isn't until 11:40.
Traveller: I'd rather go early, so that I have the whole day with her. So, the 7:25. Which platform does it leave from? I remember the station being quite confusing.
Clerk: It can be. Most trains to the coast go from platform two, but this one doesn't. The departure platform is six. That's over the footbridge, at the far end of the station, so give yourself at least ten minutes to get there.
Traveller: Platform six. And just to check, it really is direct? I don't want to end up stuck somewhere.
Clerk: Yes, it is. You will not need to change trains on that service. The only thing to be careful about is that the train divides at Bramley, so make sure you're sitting in the front four carriages. There are signs on the platform.
Traveller: The front four carriages. OK. Can I reserve a seat? I'd love to see the sea on the way.
Clerk: Let me check. The table seats have all gone, I'm afraid, but a reserved window seat is still available. It's on the left-hand side, so you'll have a view of the coast for the last half hour.
Traveller: Wonderful, I'll take that. And how much will the ticket be? I'll be coming back on Sunday evening.
Clerk: If you travelled at peak times, a return would be 46 pounds. But both your trains are outside the busy period, so you can have the cheaper ticket. The off-peak return costs 28 pounds.
Traveller: That's much better than I expected.

### 7-10
Traveller: There's one more thing. I'd like to take my bike, so I can cycle around while I'm there. Is that allowed?
Clerk: It depends on the type of bike. Folding bikes can go on any train, free of charge. Is yours a folding one?
Traveller: No, it's an ordinary mountain bike. Quite a big one, actually.
Clerk: In that case, you'll need to book. Large bicycles require a separate reservation. There's space for four on each train. It's free, but you do need to book it in advance. I can do that for you now.
Traveller: Yes, please. Is there anything else I should know about the journey?
Clerk: Yes, there is something. Current engineering work affects the coastal line. That's the stretch between Bramley and Westhaven. It won't stop your train running, but it'll go more slowly through that section, so you might arrive ten or fifteen minutes late. The inland route isn't affected, but that's the stopping service, which takes longer anyway.
Traveller: That's OK. I'll warn my grandmother. Now, how do I get the ticket? Will you post it to me?
Clerk: We don't post tickets any more. You can collect the ticket from a machine at any station. You just need the card you paid with and your booking reference. The booking office here is only open until six, and the station kiosk only sells snacks and newspapers, so the machine is the easiest option.
Traveller: Fine. And if there are any changes because of the engineering work, how will I find out? Will someone phone me?
Clerk: We don't call customers individually, I'm afraid, and a letter wouldn't reach you in time. Updates are sent by text message, so I'll just need your mobile number. You'll get a message if your train is delayed or cancelled.
Traveller: Great. It's 07700 900 418.
Clerk: Thank you. Right, that's all booked. Have a lovely trip.

## Part 2 | Local History Festival
intro: an organiser describing the programme for a town history festival
voices: Host=Charon

### 11-16
Host: Good evening, and thank you all for coming to this information session. I'm Graham Wells, chair of the Ashford Local History Society, and I'm delighted to tell you about this year's Ashford History Festival. This is our tenth festival, so it's a special year for us, and we've planned a bigger programme than ever before.
Host: For those of you who are new to the town, the festival is a celebration of Ashford's past, from its origins as a small river crossing to its growth as a busy market town in the nineteenth century. It's organised entirely by volunteers, and it's supported by the town council and by local businesses.
Host: Let me start with the dates. In previous years, the festival took place at the end of June, but this year we've moved it a week earlier to avoid clashing with the county show. The festival opens on 18 June, and it runs for three days, finishing on the Sunday evening.
Host: As for the location, in the past, events were spread out all over the town, and visitors told us it was difficult to find their way around. So this year we've brought almost everything together. Most events take place in the market square, where we'll have a large marquee and a stage. A few talks will still be held in the town hall, which is right next to the square.
Host: Each day follows a similar pattern. The morning begins with a storytelling walk. One of our volunteers, dressed in costume, will lead you through the old streets and tell stories about the people who lived and worked there. The walks last about an hour, and they're suitable for all ages.
Host: We're particularly keen to attract families this year, so there are plenty of activities for children. In honour of Ashford's famous stone bridge, which was built in 1780, children can build a model bridge out of card and wooden sticks, and then test how much weight it can carry. There'll be prizes for the strongest bridges each day.
Host: Then, in the afternoons, there's a range of workshops where you can see traditional crafts. On Friday there's a demonstration of rope-making, but the most popular event last year was the one in the old printing works, so we're repeating it. The afternoon workshop demonstrates bookbinding, and visitors will be able to make a small notebook to take home.
Host: You may be wondering about the cost. The society has been very fortunate to receive a grant from the council this year. General admission is free, so you can walk around the square and join most of the activities without paying anything. There's a small charge for one or two special events, which I'll come to in a moment.

### 17-20
Host: Now, some events have limited space, so it's important to plan ahead.
Host: One of the highlights of this year's festival is a guided tour of the town archive, which is normally closed to the public. You'll see original maps, letters and photographs going back three hundred years. The archive tour needs advance booking, because the rooms are small and we can only take ten people at a time. It costs five pounds.
Host: On each evening, there's a talk on the main stage. Last year's talks were about local poetry and industrial design, and they were very well attended. This year, the speaker is Doctor Anna Price from the university, and the evening talk covers river trade. She'll explain how goods such as wool and coal were transported along the river before the railway arrived.
Host: We want the festival to be accessible to everyone. The main entrance to the town hall has steps, and unfortunately there isn't a lift in the building. However, a step-free entrance is available behind the hall, and there'll be a volunteer there throughout the festival to help.
Host: Finally, you'll want to know where to get more information. Festival leaflets are available in most shops in town, but they only give a summary. A full programme is available at the library desk, with the times, locations and details of every event. Please don't ask at the tourist kiosk, as they don't have copies this year.
Host: Thank you very much. I'm happy to take any questions now.

## Part 3 | Peer Assessment Debate
intro: two trainee teachers, Olivia and Marcus, planning a small study about peer assessment
voices: Olivia=Kore, Marcus=Puck

### 21-26
Olivia: Marcus, have you got a minute? I wanted to talk about our action research project before we send the proposal to our mentor.
Marcus: Of course. I've been thinking about it quite a lot, actually. I'm still not sure we've got the focus right.
Olivia: Well, the general topic is peer assessment, students giving each other feedback on their work. But I think we need to say exactly what we're measuring. Lots of studies look at whether students enjoy it, and that's not really our question.
Marcus: No. We want to measure the usefulness of peer feedback. Whether it actually helps students improve their work.
Olivia: Exactly. And one thing I noticed on my teaching placement is that students are often quite nervous about it. When they get comments from classmates, they feel attacked. Students often confuse correction with criticism. They think that if someone points out a mistake, it means they're being criticised personally.
Marcus: That's true. So we need to prepare them properly before they start. Shall we just give them a list of criteria?
Olivia: Yes, but I don't think we should go straight to the real assessment. We will begin with a practice rubric, a simple version with just three criteria, and let them try it on an anonymous example from last year.
Marcus: Good idea. What kind of task should they assess? I was thinking of an essay.
Olivia: Essays take so long to read, though. In a single lesson they'd only get through one. The sample task is a short presentation, about three minutes long. That way, everyone can present and get feedback in the same lesson.
Marcus: OK. But if they're presenting in front of each other, they'll know whose work they're assessing.
Olivia: Hmm, that's a problem. We could ask them to record their presentations first, and then share the videos without names. Learners should exchange work anonymously if possible. Otherwise they might not be honest.
Marcus: Right. And then how do we know whether the feedback was useful?
Olivia: Well, after they get the feedback, they'll have a chance to improve their presentation. We need to track the quality of revisions. We compare the first version with the second, and see what's changed.
Marcus: That makes sense.

### 27-30
Marcus: Now, our mentor asked us to think about the problems with peer assessment, so we should include a section on that.
Olivia: Yes. What did you have in mind?
Marcus: Well, the obvious one is that students in the same class know each other well. Friendship may influence the scores. If you're assessing your best friend, you're going to be generous, even if the work is anonymous. You might recognise their voice, for example.
Olivia: That's true. We should mention it. The other limitation is time, of course. Our placement finishes at the end of the month.
Marcus: I know. The study lasts only two sessions, so we can't say anything about long-term effects. We'd originally hoped to do follow-up interviews as well, but there just isn't time.
Olivia: We'll be clear about that in the proposal. Now, how will we check that the marking is reliable? We can't mark everything ourselves as well.
Marcus: No. Checking all the drafts would take weeks. I asked about this, and our mentor will check a random sample, five presentations from each class, and compare her marks with the students' marks.
Olivia: Great. And the last thing is how we evaluate the comments themselves. I was going to count how many words students wrote, but I'm not sure length tells us much.
Marcus: No, it doesn't. A long comment can be completely vague. I think the key question is whether the student can actually act on it. We should evaluate the feedback's specificity, not only its tone. Good job is friendly, but it isn't specific. Speak more slowly in the introduction is something you can actually do.
Olivia: I agree. OK, I'll add that and send the whole thing to our mentor tonight.

## Part 4 | Safe Cycle Junctions
intro: part of a lecture about designing safe road junctions for cyclists
voices: Lecturer=Leda

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good afternoon. In this lecture, I'm going to look at one of the most difficult problems in transport planning: how to design junctions that are safe for cyclists. As many of you will know, cycling has grown rapidly in cities over the past decade, and most governments want to encourage it further, for environmental and health reasons. But surveys consistently show that the main reason people give for not cycling is fear of traffic, and the place where that fear is greatest is at junctions, where roads meet and different road users have to cross each other's paths.
Lecturer: The statistics support this. In most cities, more than half of all serious collisions involving cyclists happen at or near junctions. So what makes a junction dangerous, and what can designers do about it?
Lecturer: Let me start with a key concept. Every time two routes cross, merge or separate, there's a point where road users might collide. Engineers call these conflict points. A simple crossroads between two-way streets has a surprisingly large number of them, more than thirty if you include cyclists and pedestrians. The crucial aim is to reduce conflict points between road users, either by removing some movements altogether, or by separating them in space or in time.
Lecturer: The second principle concerns speed. It's not only the number of collisions that matters, but how serious they are. Vehicle speed influences the severity of many collisions. A cyclist hit by a car at twenty miles an hour is very likely to survive, but at forty the chances are much lower. So junction design often includes features that slow vehicles down, such as raised crossings or tighter corners.
Lecturer: Tighter corners, however, have to be designed carefully. Drivers and cyclists need to be able to see each other in good time. Designers avoid sharp corners that obstruct sightlines, for example where a wall, a parked van or even a hedge hides an approaching cyclist from a lorry driver who is turning.
Lecturer: Another approach is to use traffic signals to separate movements in time. One of the most dangerous situations is when a vehicle turns across the path of a cyclist who is going straight ahead. A separate signal phase can protect turning movements, so that cyclists get a green light while turning traffic waits, and the other way round. The disadvantage is that it can increase waiting times for everyone.
Lecturer: Of course, junctions must work for pedestrians too. Wide junctions can be very difficult to cross, especially for older people or for parents with young children. Pedestrians benefit from a clearly marked refuge island in the middle of the road, which allows them to cross one direction of traffic at a time.
Lecturer: Some junctions are inevitably complicated, with several lanes and multiple routes. In these situations, cyclists can easily become confused about where they should be. Coloured surface markings can guide cyclists through complex areas. Many cities use green or red paint to show the cycle route clearly across the junction.
Lecturer: So how do engineers know whether a junction is dangerous in the first place? Traditionally, they relied on police records of collisions. The problem is that serious collisions are, fortunately, relatively rare, so it can take years for a pattern to appear. For this reason, engineers collect near-miss reports as well as collision records. Some cities have set up websites where cyclists can report incidents in which they were almost hit. Information about the weather and traffic volumes is also useful, but near-misses give the earliest warning.
Lecturer: Designers also have to think about who is using the cycle routes. In the past, junctions were designed around a standard bicycle ridden by a fit adult. But that's changing. Routes must also work for cargo bikes, which are increasingly used for deliveries and for carrying children. These are much longer and wider than ordinary bicycles, and they need more space to turn.
Lecturer: Finally, some problems are less obvious. A junction can be well designed in every other respect and still be dangerous because of what happens to the road surface. Poor drainage can make a junction unsafe after rain, as puddles hide potholes and force cyclists to swerve into the traffic.
Lecturer: To sum up, a junction may look attractive, with new paving and planting, but that isn't the point. A successful design is judged by safety, not appearance alone. Next week, we'll look at some examples from the Netherlands and Denmark, where these principles have been applied for many years.
