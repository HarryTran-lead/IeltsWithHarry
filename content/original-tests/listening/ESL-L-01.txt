# ESL-L-01 | Original Listening Test 01

## Part 1 | Bicycle Library Membership
intro: a telephone conversation between a library clerk and a caller who wants to borrow a bicycle for a weekend trip
voices: Clerk=Kore, Caller=Puck

### 1-6
Clerk: Good morning, Eastfield Neighbourhood Library, bicycle desk. This is Megan speaking. How can I help?
Caller: Oh, hello. I'm ringing about borrowing a bicycle for the weekend. I joined your bike-sharing scheme a while ago, but I've never actually used it, and I'm planning a day out with my sister on Saturday.
Clerk: That's no problem at all. Lots of people sign up and then forget about it until the weather improves. Could I take your surname first, please?
Caller: Yes, it's Harrington. H-A-R-R-I-N-G-T-O-N.
Clerk: Thank you. And your first name?
Caller: Daniel.
Clerk: Right, I've found you. Daniel Harrington, registered in March. Now, you'll need your membership number whenever you book, so it's worth keeping it somewhere safe. The membership reference I can see is B48-219.
Caller: Sorry, was that B for bicycle?
Clerk: That's right. B, then four, eight, then a dash, and two, one, nine.
Caller: Got it. I think I had it written on an old card, but I've no idea where that is now.
Clerk: Don't worry, it's on the system. So, which day were you hoping to pick the bike up? We like people to collect the day before a trip, so they can check everything properly.
Caller: Well, the leaflet I've got says collections are on Thursdays, so I was going to come in on Thursday after work.
Clerk: Ah, I'm afraid that leaflet is out of date. We changed the arrangements last month. The bike can be collected on Friday, not Thursday as the old leaflet says.
Caller: Friday's actually better for me anyway. Is that from the main desk on the ground floor?
Clerk: Not on Fridays, no. The building is used for evening classes, so things work a little differently. Please come to the north entrance because the main counter closes early. There's a small office just inside the door, next to the bike racks.
Caller: The north entrance. Is that the one facing the car park?
Clerk: No, the car park's on the south side. The north entrance is the one on Mill Street, opposite the bakery.
Caller: Oh, I know it. OK. And is there a charge for borrowing the bike? I seem to remember it was free for members.
Clerk: The loan itself is free, yes. But we do ask for a deposit to cover any damage. There is a refundable deposit of 30 pounds. You get it back as long as the bike comes back in the same condition it went out.
Caller: That's fine. Can I pay that by card?
Clerk: Yes, card or cash, either is fine.
Caller: And what about safety equipment? I don't actually own a helmet.
Clerk: That's not a problem. We provide a helmet at no extra charge. We've got three sizes, so when you come in we can make sure it fits properly.
Caller: Great. My sister has her own, so it'll just be one for me.
Clerk: I'll make a note of that. And one more thing about the collection. Some people bring their own lock, but our insurance doesn't cover that. You must take our combination lock with you. The code is printed on a label under the saddle, so you won't need to remember it.
Caller: That's sensible. I'd probably forget a code, to be honest.

### 7-10
Clerk: Now, let me tell you about returning the bike, because that's where people often get confused.
Caller: Yes, I wanted to ask about that. My leaflet says the bikes need to be back by five thirty.
Clerk: That used to be the case, but since we extended our opening hours, it's later. Please bring the bicycle back by six thirty in the evening. And that's on the Sunday, unless you've arranged a longer loan.
Caller: Six thirty on Sunday. That gives us plenty of time. What if we're running late?
Clerk: Then just phone us. We're in the building until seven thirty, but that last hour is for staff to lock up, so we can't take any returns then.
Caller: Understood. Now, we were thinking of cycling out to Brookfield Lake. Do you have any advice about the route? A friend said the quickest way is along the ring road.
Clerk: It is quick, but there's a lot of heavy traffic, especially on Saturdays. I wouldn't recommend it for leisure cycling. The safest route is beside the canal rather than the ring road. The towpath is flat and well surfaced, and it takes you almost all the way to the lake.
Caller: What about the path along the harbour? I've heard that's nice.
Clerk: It's very pretty, but it's closed at the moment for repairs to the sea wall, so the canal is really your best option.
Caller: The canal, then. I'll look at a map tonight. Is there anything I need to bring with me on Friday?
Clerk: Yes, there is. The bikes are stored in a locked shed, so we give you a key for the shed as well as the bike. Bring a photo ID when you collect the key. A passport or a driving licence is fine.
Caller: Would my library card do? It's got my name on it.
Clerk: I'm afraid not, because it doesn't have a photograph on it. And we don't need a paper receipt or anything like that, since your booking is on our system.
Caller: OK, so just photo ID. And if something comes up and we can't go, what should I do? It might rain, for example.
Clerk: That's a good question. We have a waiting list at weekends, so we do need some notice. For a cancellation, call at least 24 hours ahead. That way we can offer the bike to someone else.
Caller: I'm sure the old rule was 12 hours.
Clerk: It was, but too many bikes were left unused, so we changed it. For group bookings of more than four bikes it's actually 48 hours, but that won't apply to you.
Caller: No, it's just me. Right, I think that's everything. Thanks so much for your help.
Clerk: You're welcome, Daniel. Enjoy your ride, and we'll see you on Friday.

## Part 2 | Riverside Heritage Walk
intro: a community volunteer introducing a new walking route beside the river
voices: Guide=Charon

### 11-16
Guide: Good morning, everyone, and welcome to the launch of the new Riverside Heritage Walk. My name's Patrick Doyle, and I'm one of the volunteers from the Riverside Community Trust who helped to plan and mark out the route. Over the next few minutes I'll give you an overview of the walk, point out a few things to look out for, and explain some practical arrangements before you set off.
Guide: First of all, a little background. For most of the last century, this part of the river was lined with warehouses, boatyards and small factories. When the industry moved away, much of the land was left empty, and for a long time people simply didn't come down here. Over the past ten years, the trust has worked with the council to clear the paths, restore some of the old buildings and put up information boards, so that residents and visitors can enjoy the riverside again.
Guide: Now, I'd like you to look at the map you've been given. The walk begins outside the old station, which you can see in the bottom left-hand corner. It's no longer used by trains, of course. It's now a community centre. From the station, you walk north for a short distance until you reach the river, and then you turn right onto the riverside path, so that you're heading east, with the water on your left. The full route covers four kilometres, and most people complete it in about an hour and a half.
Guide: Almost immediately, you come to the first landmark. The stone bridge crosses the river just past the point where you join the riverside path. It was built in the eighteenth century and is the oldest surviving crossing in the town. You may be tempted to walk over it, but the building directly across the bridge on the north bank is a private house, so please stay on the south side of the river.
Guide: Carry on along the path, and a short distance after the bridge, right at the water's edge, you'll see a small wooden shelter. That's the bird hide. We've recorded herons, ducks and even otters along this stretch, but the bird most people hope to see is quite small and very colourful. Look for kingfishers near the water at dawn. They're very shy, so you'll need to be patient, and quiet.
Guide: As you continue east, you'll see the car park on your right, set back from the river near the road. Families will be pleased to know that there's a new play area for young children. It's on the right of the path, between the path and the car park, so it's easy to find if you've come by car.
Guide: On the other side of the path from the play area, right on the riverbank, is one of the old boatsheds, which has now been restored. A small boat museum opens after eleven inside it. It's run entirely by volunteers, and it has a fascinating collection of model barges and the tools used by local boat builders.
Guide: A little further on, you'll reach the footbridge. It's worth crossing it, because the viewing platform is at the far end of the footbridge, on the north bank. From there, you get the best view back along the river towards the stone bridge. The other building you can see on the north bank, further west, is the old ferry office, but that isn't open to the public.
Guide: When you've crossed back over, follow the path as it bends away from the river towards the south-east. There are benches beside the willow grove, at the point where the path first turns away from the water and goes into the trees. It's a lovely shady spot for a rest, and from there the path takes you through the woods to the customs house, where the walk ends.

### 17-20
Guide: Now I'd like to mention a few practical points about the route as a whole.
Guide: Many of you will have walked the eastern section before, which is a paved lane suitable for pushchairs and wheelchairs. The western half is rather different. We considered building a wooden track there, but it would have been too expensive to maintain so close to the water. The western section uses a gravel path, so wear suitable shoes. It can be a little uneven, especially after heavy rain.
Guide: You should also be aware that some building work is going on at the moment. The old mill is being converted into flats, and the area around it isn't safe for the public. A temporary diversion avoids the mill yard. Just follow the orange arrows. The diversion takes you past the repair yard, where the council keeps its boats, and then back to the river at the market bridge, so you won't miss any of the views.
Guide: Of course, a walk wouldn't be complete without somewhere to stop for a drink. There used to be a cafe called Willow Corner near the grove, but sadly it closed last year. And the Riverside Table restaurant further on is only open in the evenings. So if you want refreshments, plan ahead. The last cafe is called Harbour Cup. It's right at the end of the route, next to the old customs house, and it serves excellent homemade cakes.
Guide: Finally, a word about our volunteers. They'll be stationed at several points along the route today, wearing yellow jackets, and they'll be happy to answer any questions. If anything goes missing on the walk, a bag, a hat, even a dog lead, please don't take it to the museum lobby, because there's very little space there. And the ticket window at the station is only open on weekdays. Lost property should be reported at the information tent. You'll find it beside the starting point all weekend.
Guide: I think that's everything. Do enjoy the walk, and please remember to take your litter home with you. Thank you.

## Part 3 | Research on Shared Desks
intro: two students, Lucy and Ahmed, discussing their research project on shared study spaces
voices: Lucy=Aoede, Ahmed=Orus

### 21-26
Lucy: Hi Ahmed. Thanks for staying behind. I thought we should go through our project on shared desks before we write up the final report.
Ahmed: Yes, good idea. The deadline's only two weeks away, and I'm still not completely sure how we're framing the whole thing.
Lucy: Well, I think we need to start by stating the research question really clearly, because in our first draft it was a bit vague. Our main research question concerns concentration, rather than examination scores.
Ahmed: Right. I remember our tutor pointed out that grades depend on so many other things, like how much people revise and how difficult the course is, that we'd never be able to link them to where someone sits.
Lucy: Exactly. Whereas concentration is something students can actually report on from day to day. So, next, the participants. How should we describe them?
Ahmed: Well, we recruited first-year students from three programmes. That was history, chemistry and business studies.
Lucy: And we should explain why we chose first-years.
Ahmed: Because they're the ones who use the shared study rooms most. Second- and third-year students tend to work in the departmental libraries.
Lucy: Good. Then there's the data collection. I know we originally planned to send out a long online questionnaire.
Ahmed: Yes, but we dropped that idea after the trial version. People just gave up halfway through. The main evidence came from short diaries, not a long survey. Each participant wrote a few lines at the end of every study session.
Lucy: And the diaries were much richer than I expected. Some people wrote quite detailed comments about what had distracted them.
Ahmed: Mm. But there's a weakness we have to acknowledge. We didn't choose who took part. Students volunteered. Our tutor warned that self-selection could bias the sample. People who already enjoy working in shared rooms might have been more willing to join.
Lucy: That's a fair point. I'll put it in the limitations section. And we should be honest about the timescale too.
Ahmed: Yes. We originally hoped to run the study for a whole term, but in the end the pilot ran for three weeks. That's probably enough to spot some patterns, but not to draw firm conclusions.
Lucy: Agreed. Now, what about the results? Was there anything that surprised you?
Ahmed: Definitely. I assumed the main problem would be people chatting, or noise from the corridor. But actually, students complained most about phone calls. People taking calls at their desks instead of going outside.
Lucy: Yes, that came up again and again in the diaries. Some people said even a quiet conversation on the phone was more distracting than a group discussion, because you only hear one side of it.

### 27-30
Ahmed: So, moving on to the suggestions that participants made. We asked them what would help them concentrate better.
Lucy: Right. A few people wanted the rooms to have extended opening hours, but that's really a separate issue. It's about access rather than concentration.
Ahmed: And I thought most people would ask for individual desks with partitions.
Lucy: Some did, but not as many as I expected. The preferred alternative was a bookable room. Somewhere a small group could reserve for an hour if they needed to discuss their work or make a call, so that the shared areas stay quiet.
Ahmed: That makes sense, given the complaints about phone calls. OK, next, the analysis. I've been looking at the times of day when people recorded their sessions.
Lucy: And?
Ahmed: Well, there were very few entries for weekend afternoons, so I don't think we can say much about those. And early evenings were quite mixed. I think the clearest contrast is between the start and the end of the working day. We should compare mornings with late afternoons.
Lucy: Yes. The late afternoon sessions seem to be when people felt most tired and most easily distracted. That would make an interesting graph.
Ahmed: Speaking of graphs, we need to think about the poster for the department conference. What did our tutor say about the draft?
Lucy: She liked the overall layout. She thought the photograph of the study room was useful, so we can keep that, and the participant chart was fine. But she said people wouldn't understand how we collected the data. The tutor suggested a clearer methods diagram. Something that shows the steps: recruitment, the diaries, and then the analysis.
Ahmed: I can do that. I'll keep it simple, maybe just four boxes with arrows.
Lucy: Perfect. And finally, the recommendation. We've gone back and forth on this one.
Ahmed: I know. At one point we were going to suggest fixed seating, so that people always sit in the same place. But the data didn't really support that. And longer sessions wouldn't help either, since people got tired.
Lucy: No. Different students need different things at different times. We agreed to recommend flexible zones rather than a universal rule. Some quiet areas, some areas for discussion, and the bookable rooms for calls.
Ahmed: I think that's the most honest conclusion. Right, shall we divide up the writing?
Lucy: Yes. I'll take the methods and the limitations, and you can do the results.

## Part 4 | Urban Tree Roots
intro: part of a lecture about how street design affects city trees
voices: Lecturer=Kore

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good afternoon. In today's lecture, I'm going to look at a topic that most people never think about when they walk down a city street: what is happening underneath the trees. We tend to admire large street trees for their shade and their appearance, and we're increasingly aware of their value in cooling cities and absorbing rainwater. But a tree's ability to survive in an urban environment depends largely on conditions below the surface, and that's what I want to focus on today.
Lecturer: Let's start with some basic biology. A common misconception is that roots simply grow downwards in search of water. In fact, most of the roots of a mature tree are found in the top metre of soil, and they spread outwards, often well beyond the edge of the branches. The reason they stay so close to the surface is that roots need oxygen as well as water. Without air in the soil, root cells can't function, and the tree gradually declines.
Lecturer: This brings us to one of the main problems in cities. When streets are built, the ground is pressed down by heavy machinery so that it can support roads and pavements. That's essential for the engineers, but it's bad news for trees. Compact soil prevents healthy root expansion. The tiny spaces between soil particles, which would normally hold air and water, are squeezed out, and the roots simply can't push through.
Lecturer: The surface above the roots matters too. Traditional pavements made of concrete or asphalt are almost completely sealed, so when it rains, the water runs straight into the drains instead of reaching the soil. One solution that many cities are now adopting is to change the material. Porous paving allows more rainfall to enter the ground. It looks quite similar to ordinary paving, but it contains gaps that let water, and air, pass through.
Lecturer: So how do we know when a street tree is struggling? Unfortunately, trees don't show damage immediately, and the signs can easily be misread. Yellowing leaves can indicate stress, though not its cause. The tree could be short of water, it could have damaged roots, or it could even have a disease, so a proper inspection is always needed before anyone decides what to do.
Lecturer: A great deal of root damage happens during construction work, often years before the tree shows any symptoms. When trenches are dug for new pipes or foundations, roots can be cut or crushed. For this reason, many cities now have strict guidelines. Workers should protect the root zone during excavation. In practice, that means putting up fencing around the tree, not parking vehicles or storing materials under the branches, and digging by hand close to the major roots.
Lecturer: Of course, in busy city centres, there isn't always room for a large area of open soil. Here, engineers have developed some clever solutions. Engineers can use structural cells below busy paths. These are plastic or metal frames, rather like crates, which hold up the pavement while leaving the soil inside them loose, so that roots have space to grow.
Lecturer: Let's turn now to the care of young trees. Planting a new tree is relatively cheap, but keeping it alive is a different matter. One mistake councils often make is to stop watering too soon. New plantings need extra water during their first two summers. After that, the roots should be established enough to find water for themselves, except in very dry years.
Lecturer: I'd like to mention a study carried out in several European cities, which tracked the success of street tree programmes over a decade. Rather than simply counting the number of trees planted, which is what most councils report, the researchers took a broader view. Teams recorded changes in canopy cover each year. In other words, they measured how much of the street was actually shaded by leaves, which is a much better indicator of the benefits that trees provide.
Lecturer: One of the clearest findings concerned the space underneath the streets. You might expect drains or the foundations of buildings to be the main obstacle, and they can be. But the study found that buried cables often limit planting space. Electricity and telecommunications cables are frequently laid close to the surface, and utility companies don't want roots growing around them, so many potential planting sites are rejected.
Lecturer: So what can we conclude? Well, the main message from the research is that planting is only the beginning. Cities that announce ambitious targets, a million new trees, for instance, often get good publicity. But if those trees die within a few years, nothing has been achieved. Successful projects require long-term maintenance, not only new trees. That means budgets for watering, inspection and pruning over many years, and close cooperation between engineers, planners and the people who look after the trees.
Lecturer: Next week, we'll look at how different species respond to the heat of city streets. Thank you.
