## Part 1 | Cookery School Booking
intro: a telephone conversation between a cookery school receptionist and a caller who wants to book a class
voices: Receptionist=Zephyr, Caller=Kore

### 1-6
Receptionist: Good morning, Greenway Cookery School. How can I help you?
Caller: Hi there. I'm interested in one of your evening cooking classes. I'm not very experienced. I can make a few basic things, but I'd like to learn to cook some proper meals.
Receptionist: Then our evening course would be ideal. It's aimed at people who want to cook healthy, simple dishes at home. The course is called Seasonal Suppers, because each week we use whatever vegetables are in season.
Caller: That sounds just right. Is it a whole course, or can I come to a single class?
Receptionist: You can book individual classes, so there's no need to sign up for the whole term. The next session is on Wednesday. That's today, in fact, and we still have two places.
Caller: Oh, today! That's short notice, but I'm free this evening. What time does it start?
Receptionist: The cooking starts at six, but we like everyone to be settled in beforehand, so please arrive at 5:45 p.m. That gives you time to put your things away and meet the chef.
Caller: Fine. And how much is a single class?
Receptionist: It used to be 30 pounds, but our prices went up in January. The fee is 36 pounds, and that includes all the ingredients. And of course, you get to eat what you've cooked.
Caller: That's reasonable. What will we be cooking tonight?
Receptionist: Each week has a different theme. Last week was all about mushrooms. Tonight the class focuses on lentils. The chef will show you three different dishes: a soup, a curry and a salad.
Caller: Lovely. I've never really known what to do with lentils.
Receptionist: Then you'll learn a lot. One thing I do need to ask, though. Do you have any problems with particular foods? Tell us about any allergies when you register, so that the chef can prepare alternatives for you. It's very important, because we sometimes use nuts and sesame.
Caller: I don't have any, luckily. How many people are there in a class?
Receptionist: Twelve at most. You'll work in pairs, so it's very sociable, and the chef can help everyone individually.

### 7-10
Caller: Do I need to bring anything with me? Knives, or special clothes?
Receptionist: No, all the equipment is provided: knives, pans, everything. And you don't need to worry about your clothes either. We supply an apron, and you can borrow a hat if you have long hair.
Caller: Great. Where exactly is the school? I've got the address, but I've never been there.
Receptionist: We're in the old warehouse on Canal Street. Our reception is on the ground level, and there's a cafe on the first floor, but don't stop there. The kitchen is on the second floor. You can take the lift or the stairs.
Caller: OK. Is there anywhere to park nearby?
Receptionist: There's a public car park behind the building, but it's quite expensive in the evenings. Most of our students come by bus. The number fourteen stops right outside.
Caller: I'll get the bus, then. And will I be eating there, or should I take the food home?
Receptionist: You'll eat some of the food at the end of the class, but there's usually quite a lot left over. You should bring a container for leftovers, so you can take some home for lunch the next day. We'll also give you a recipe card for each dish, so you can cook them again.
Caller: That's a good idea. Now, how do I pay? Can I pay now, over the phone?
Receptionist: We don't take payment over the phone, I'm afraid, and our mobile app is being updated at the moment. You can pay when you arrive this evening. Payment can be made by card. We don't accept cash any more.
Caller: That's fine. I'll bring my card. Do you need my name?
Receptionist: Yes, please. And a contact number, in case anything changes.
Caller: It's Laura Bennett. B-E-N-N-E-T-T.
Receptionist: Thank you, Laura. We'll see you at quarter to six.

## Part 2 | Farmers Market Tour
intro: a guide introducing a tour of a farmers market
voices: Guide=Puck

### 11-16
Guide: Good morning, everybody, and welcome to Riverbank Farmers Market. My name's Tom, and I'll be your guide this morning. The market was set up fifteen years ago by a group of farmers who wanted to sell directly to the public, and it's grown from just eight stalls to more than sixty. Today, I'll be showing you around and introducing some of the people who grow and make the food that's sold here.
Guide: We'll start in just a moment. If you're not already with the group, the tour starts beside the clock tower, so please make your way over here now. The tour lasts about an hour and a half, and we'll stop for a break halfway through.
Guide: Let me tell you a little about who sells here. Unlike many markets, we have strict rules about where the food comes from. Most stalls are run by local growers, which means farms within thirty miles of the city. We also have a few stalls selling things that can't be grown locally, such as coffee and olive oil, but they're clearly labelled.
Guide: Our first stop will be at the Hillside Farm stall, where Mary, the farmer, will give a short talk. The first demonstration concerns seasonal vegetables. She'll show you how to tell what's in season at different times of the year, and explain why vegetables picked at the right time taste better and need less transport and storage.
Guide: After that, we'll move to the market garden behind the main hall. This is a small growing area that we use to show visitors how vegetables can be produced without chemicals. The key is healthy soil. Visitors can examine a sample of compost, and don't worry, it doesn't smell. The gardeners will explain how it's made from the market's own food waste.
Guide: Next, we'll go to the bakery, which is one of the most popular stalls. The bread is baked on site every morning, starting at four a.m. The bakery uses a wood-fired oven, which gives the bread its thick, crispy crust. It was built by the baker himself, using bricks from an old farmhouse.
Guide: Our final stop before the break is the honey stall. A beekeeper explains the work of pollinators, not just honeybees, but also bumblebees, butterflies and other insects, and why they're so important for the crops that are sold here.

### 17-20
Guide: Before we set off, here's some practical information.
Guide: Please stay together as a group, as the market gets very busy after ten o'clock. And if you need to leave the tour early, just let me know.
Guide: When we're in the market garden, you may notice that there are no hosepipes. The market is very careful about saving water. The garden uses rainwater tanks for irrigation. They collect water from the roof of the main hall, and in a normal year that provides all the water the garden needs.
Guide: Many of you will want to try some of the food. There's a tasting session for everyone on the tour. Some of you asked whether it starts at eleven, but it's later than that. The tasting session starts at midday, in the covered area next to the bakery. Plates and cups are provided.
Guide: Of course, you'll probably want to buy some of the things you've tasted. The stallholders are trying to reduce the amount of plastic used at the market, so they no longer give out plastic bags. Please bring a reusable bag if you want to shop. If you've forgotten one, cloth bags are on sale at the information point.
Guide: And finally, we're always trying to improve the tour, so we'd love to hear what you think. We used to have a visitor notebook at the information point, but very few people wrote in it. Visitor comments go into a collection box by the main gate now. There are cards and pens next to it. We're also planning an online survey, but that isn't ready yet.
Guide: Right, if everyone's ready, let's go and meet Mary.

## Part 3 | Packaging Survey
intro: two students, Nadia and Leo, discussing their research on reusable takeaway containers
voices: Nadia=Aoede, Leo=Orus

### 21-26
Nadia: Hi Leo. Shall we go through the results of our packaging survey? We need to start writing up the report this week.
Leo: Yes, let's. I've put all the data into a spreadsheet, so we can look at it together.
Nadia: Great. First, though, I think we need to rewrite the introduction. When I read it again, it sounded as if we were studying whether people like the look of the reusable containers.
Leo: That's not what we meant at all. Our main question is about convenience rather than packaging appearance. Whether people find it easy enough to borrow a container and bring it back.
Nadia: Exactly. Next, the location. We should explain why we chose the place we did.
Leo: Well, we originally wanted to include the sandwich shop in the library, but the manager wasn't keen. In the end, we surveyed customers at the student cafe, because that's where the reusable container scheme was being trialled.
Nadia: And how many people took part in the end? I know we handed out over a hundred questionnaires.
Leo: We did, a hundred and ten, I think. But quite a lot came back half empty. We obtained seventy-two completed responses, which is still enough for a basic analysis.
Nadia: OK. So, what were people most worried about? I expected it to be hygiene.
Leo: Some people did mention hygiene, but it wasn't the main issue. The most common concern was the deposit. People have to pay two pounds when they borrow a container, and they get it back when they return it. A lot of students felt that was too much, or they worried about losing it.
Nadia: Interesting. And what about returning the containers? That was the part of the scheme that seemed to cause problems.
Leo: Yes. At the moment, you can only return them to the cafe, which is at the edge of the campus. Participants wanted a return point near lecture rooms, so they could drop off their containers on the way to class.
Nadia: That makes sense. And there was another comment that came up a lot. People weren't sure whether the containers were properly cleaned.
Leo: That's right. Actually, they're washed in a commercial dishwasher at very high temperatures, but nobody knows that. We should explain the washing process more clearly. Maybe with a poster at the counter.

### 27-30
Nadia: OK. Now, let's think about the method section. We need to describe the pilot scheme itself.
Leo: Right. The cafe originally planned to run it for three months, but they ran out of containers, so it was shorter than that. The pilot operated for six weeks, from the start of October to the middle of November.
Nadia: And we did our survey during the last five days of the pilot. Now, one thing I think is a real strength of our study is that we didn't just ask people what they thought.
Leo: Yes. Asking people whether they'd use the scheme isn't enough, because people always say they'll do the right thing. We counted actual returns as well as stated intentions. The cafe recorded every container that was borrowed and returned.
Nadia: And what did that show?
Leo: Well, about ninety per cent of people said they'd always return their container, but only around seventy per cent actually did.
Nadia: That's a big gap. What about the weekends? The cafe's open on Saturdays, isn't it?
Leo: Yes, but very few students use it then, and most of the weekend customers are visitors who don't know about the scheme. So weekend orders were excluded from the main analysis. We can mention them briefly in an appendix.
Nadia: Good. And then the conclusion. We should make some recommendations to the cafe.
Leo: Yes, but I think we need to be balanced. It's not just about the customers. When I spoke to the cafe manager, she said the scheme created a lot of extra work: checking containers, washing them and keeping records. The conclusion should mention staff workload, because if the staff can't manage it, the scheme won't continue.
Nadia: I agree. OK, I'll start drafting the method section tonight.

## Part 4 | Microbes in the Soil
intro: part of a lecture about the living organisms found in soil
voices: Lecturer=Charon

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning. Today I'm going to talk about something that's literally under our feet, but which scientists have only begun to understand in the last few decades: the life in soil. To most people, soil is just dirt, a mixture of minerals that plants grow in. But a single handful of healthy soil can contain more living organisms than there are people on Earth. These include bacteria, fungi, and tiny animals such as worms and mites. Today I'll look at where these organisms live, what they do, and how scientists study them.
Lecturer: Let's begin with the physical structure of soil. If you look at soil under a microscope, you'll see that it isn't solid. It consists of mineral particles of different sizes, sand, silt and clay, together with decaying plant material, and between them is a network of gaps filled with air and water. Many organisms occupy the tiny pore spaces between particles, where they're protected from drying out and from larger predators.
Lecturer: So what do all these organisms actually do? One of their most important roles is recycling. When leaves fall or plants die, the nutrients they contain are locked up in complex compounds that plants can't use directly. Microbes help release nutrients from organic matter, breaking it down into simpler forms, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, which plant roots can absorb.
Lecturer: Fungi play a particularly interesting role. Unlike bacteria, which are single cells, many soil fungi grow as long, thin structures. Fungal threads can extend through the soil for considerable distances, connecting different plants and carrying water and nutrients between them. Some scientists have described this as an underground communication network, although that idea is still debated.
Lecturer: Soil communities are not fixed. They change constantly in response to conditions. Temperature is one factor, but water is even more important. Changes in moisture alter which organisms are active. After heavy rain, some bacteria multiply rapidly, while in dry periods many organisms become inactive and wait for better conditions.
Lecturer: Air is equally important. Most soil organisms, like plant roots, need oxygen to survive. When soil is pressed down by heavy machinery, or even by people walking repeatedly over the same area, the pore spaces are squeezed. Compacted ground can limit oxygen movement, and the organisms that depend on it gradually disappear.
Lecturer: Now, how do scientists study organisms that are far too small to see? For a long time, the main method was to grow them in the laboratory. The problem is that the vast majority of soil microbes simply won't grow in laboratory conditions. Today, a laboratory may compare samples using DNA analysis, which identifies organisms from the genetic material they leave in the soil, even if they can't be grown.
Lecturer: Using these techniques, researchers have discovered that soil is far from uniform. Even within a single field, conditions can vary greatly over a few centimetres. Plants have a big influence on this. Different roots create different microhabitats, because each plant species releases its own mixture of sugars and other substances into the soil around it, and these attract particular organisms.
Lecturer: This variety has important implications for agriculture. Traditional farming often involves ploughing the soil every year, which helps to control weeds and prepare the ground for seeds. But it has a cost. Excessive disturbance may disrupt soil structure, breaking up fungal networks and the small clumps of soil that hold air and water. This is why some farmers are now experimenting with less ploughing.
Lecturer: I want to add a word of caution about how we interpret the data. It's tempting to assume that more microbes means better soil. But a high microbial count does not prove high soil health. A sudden increase in certain bacteria, for example, could be a sign of pollution, or of a disease affecting the plants. What matters is the balance between different organisms, and the functions they perform.
Lecturer: And this brings me to the direction the field is now taking. In the past, scientists tended to focus on one organism at a time. Now the focus is shifting. Researchers study relationships among organisms, not just individual species: how bacteria, fungi, plants and animals interact, compete and depend on one another. In the next lecture, we'll look at some of these relationships in more detail.
