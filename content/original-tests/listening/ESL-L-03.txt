## Part 1 | Makerspace Registration
intro: a parent phoning a community makerspace to book a weekend workshop for a teenager
voices: Receptionist=Kore, Parent=Orus

### 1-6
Receptionist: Good morning, Northgate Makerspace. Jenny speaking.
Parent: Oh, hello. I'm phoning about your weekend workshops for teenagers. My son's very interested in electronics, and a friend told me you run classes for beginners.
Receptionist: Yes, we do. We have several workshops for young people at the moment. How old is your son?
Parent: He's thirteen. He's done a little bit of coding at school, but nothing with electronics.
Receptionist: Then I'd recommend our introductory course. The beginner class is called Paper Circuits. The students make greetings cards and small models that light up, using simple circuits drawn on paper. It's a really good way to learn the basics.
Parent: Paper Circuits. That sounds fun. When does it run? He has football on Sunday mornings, so that wouldn't work.
Receptionist: That's fine, because it's not on a Sunday. It takes place on Saturday. The next one is this coming weekend, and then there's another at the end of the month.
Parent: This weekend would be great, if there's still space. What time does it start?
Receptionist: There are still four places left. The doors open at ten, and the session begins at ten fifteen. It finishes at half past twelve.
Parent: OK. And how much does it cost?
Receptionist: The standard fee is 18 pounds. For members of the makerspace it's a bit cheaper, 15 pounds, but you'd need to join first, and membership is 40 pounds a year.
Parent: I think we'll just pay the standard fee for now, and see whether he enjoys it. Is there an age limit? His younger sister might want to come along as well. She's ten.
Receptionist: I'm afraid she's a little too young for this class. We use some small tools and hot glue guns, so children must be twelve or older. We do have a craft session for younger children on the first Saturday of every month, though.
Parent: I'll mention it to her. Does he need to bring any materials? I wasn't sure whether we'd have to buy batteries or anything like that.
Receptionist: No, everything's included in the price. The batteries, the small lights, which are called LEDs, and the copper tape, which is what the students use to make the circuits. All copper tape is supplied, so you don't need to buy anything at all.
Parent: That's good to hear. I had a look online, and some of those kits are quite expensive.
Receptionist: They can be. If he enjoys the class, the tutor can recommend a good starter kit for home.

### 7-10

Receptionist: First, because your son is under sixteen, we need permission from a parent or guardian. We need a signed consent form. I can email it to you today, and he can bring it with him, or you can sign it when you drop him off.
Parent: I'll print it out and sign it at home. Where is the workshop exactly? I've only been to your cafe downstairs.
Receptionist: The workshop meets in the green studio. It's on the first floor. When you come in, go past the cafe and up the stairs, and it's the second door on the left. We also have a blue studio up there, but that's used for the 3D printing class.
Parent: The green studio. Got it. Will there be a break? He gets hungry quite quickly.
Receptionist: There's a short break halfway through. We don't provide food, and the cafe is closed to students during the session, so participants should bring a small snack. Please don't send anything with nuts in it, as some of our students have allergies.
Parent: Can he bring a drink as well?
Receptionist: Yes, a bottle of water is a good idea. There's also a water fountain in the corridor.
Parent: OK. And at the end of the session, where should I collect him? Do I come up to the studio?
Receptionist: No, we ask parents not to come upstairs, because the next class will be setting up. And the reception area gets very crowded at lunchtime. Parents collect students at the side door. It's on Wells Street, just round the corner from the main entrance. A member of staff will bring the students down the side staircase and wait with them there.
Parent: That sounds very organised. Right, I think that's everything. Can I pay now?
Receptionist: Of course. I'll just need a few details first.

## Part 2 | Museum Sound Exhibition
intro: a museum curator introducing a new exhibition about the history of recorded sound
voices: Curator=Leda

### 11-16
Curator: Good afternoon, everyone, and welcome to the City Museum. My name is Helen Brooks, and I'm the curator of our new exhibition, Voices from the Past, which opened last week. It tells the story of recorded sound, from the very first experiments in the nineteenth century to the digital recordings we use today. Before you go in, I'd like to give you a brief introduction and explain how the exhibition works.
Curator: First of all, where to find it. Some of you may have been to our previous exhibitions in the main hall on the ground floor, but this one is upstairs. The exhibition is in Gallery Four, on the second floor. You can take the lift, or the stairs beside the shop.
Curator: The exhibition is arranged in chronological order, so you'll begin with the earliest recording technology. Before records and tapes, sound was stored on hollow tubes made of a soft material that could be marked by a needle. The first display shows a wax cylinder. This particular one was recorded in 1890, and it contains a short speech by a local politician. It's one of the oldest recordings in the country.
Curator: Of course, an exhibition about sound wouldn't be complete if you couldn't hear anything. At each display there's a listening point where you can hear the recordings. We considered using loudspeakers, but with so many visitors the noise would be overwhelming. So visitors should use the provided headphones. They're cleaned after every use. Your own headphones won't connect to our system, so there's no need to bring them.
Curator: Many of you have booked a place on a guided visit. These run every hour, starting on the hour. The guided visit lasts forty minutes, which leaves you some time afterwards to explore on your own, or to visit the cafe.
Curator: Some of the older recordings are quite difficult to hear clearly, because of the hiss and crackle on the original material. We've done our best to clean them up, but we know that some visitors find them hard to follow, and some have hearing difficulties. So, a printed transcript is available for each recording. You'll find them in the folders at each listening point.
Curator: Halfway through the exhibition, you'll come to the interactive area, which is very popular with younger visitors. There are several stations where you can experiment with sound. The main interactive station demonstrates echo. You stand inside a curved chamber, speak into a tube, and hear your voice come back to you several times, just as it would in a cave or a large empty building.

### 17-20
Curator: Now, a few practical points before you go in.
Curator: Many of the items in the exhibition are very old and easily damaged. Fragile objects are displayed behind glass, so please don't lean on the cases. Some visitors have asked whether the sound effects might damage the objects, but don't worry, the volume is carefully controlled.
Curator: We also have a special programme for families. At weekends, family tours include a sound hunt. Children are given a list of sounds to find in the exhibition, such as a steam train, a bird singing and a telephone ringing, and if they find them all, they get a small prize at the end. Parents sometimes ask about the listening bench in the entrance hall. That's simply a place to sit and listen to music, and it isn't part of the tour.
Curator: A question we're often asked is whether visitors can take pictures. We're happy for you to do so. Photography is allowed without flash, because a bright flash can damage some of the older materials, especially the paper labels. Some schools also bring drawing materials, which is fine, as long as you use pencils rather than paint.
Curator: The exhibition ends with a section about the future of sound recording, including a short video about how old recordings are being preserved in digital form. But that's not quite the end of your visit. The final stop is the archive desk, where our staff can help you search the museum's collection of more than ten thousand recordings. If you have old recordings at home, they can also give you advice on looking after them.
Curator: Right, I think that's all. The first group can follow me now. Enjoy the exhibition.

## Part 3 | Note Taking Seminar
intro: two students, Emma and Ryan, discussing methods of taking notes in lectures
voices: Emma=Zephyr, Ryan=Puck

### 21-26
Ryan: Hi Emma. So, we need to plan our presentation for the study skills seminar. Did you manage to try out one of the note-taking methods we talked about?
Emma: Yes, I did. I tried the Cornell method for two weeks. You know, the one where you divide the page into a narrow column on the left and a wider one on the right, with a space for a summary at the bottom.
Ryan: How did you get on with it?
Emma: Mixed, really. Writing notes in the main column was fine, because that's basically what I did before. My biggest problem was abbreviations I could not remember. I was trying to write so quickly that I invented short forms for everything, and when I read my notes a few days later, I had no idea what half of them meant.
Ryan: I've had that problem too. Did anything work well?
Emma: Yes. The narrow column on the left is meant for questions and key words that you add after the lecture. I found that really useful. The review column made summaries easier, because I'd already picked out the main points, so writing the summary at the bottom only took about five minutes.
Ryan: That's interesting. I didn't try the Cornell method myself. I just kept taking ordinary notes, so that we'd have something to compare it with.
Emma: And I also asked my lab partner, Sam, to try something different. My partner preferred a mind map for visual topics, things like the structure of a cell, where you can draw connections between the parts. But he said it didn't work so well for lectures that were mostly about processes in time order.
Ryan: That's a useful point for the presentation. Did you talk to our tutor about any of this?
Emma: Yes, briefly. I told her I was trying to write down everything the lecturer said. The tutor recommended recording key ideas rather than every sentence. She said that if you write everything, you don't actually think about what you're hearing.
Ryan: I suppose that's true. Now, we need to describe how we tested the methods. We both used the same lecture, didn't we?
Emma: Yes. We tested our methods in a biology lecture, the one on genetics, because we're both taking that module. We thought about using one of the literature lectures, but you're not on that course.
Ryan: Right. And a physics tutorial wouldn't have worked, because it's mostly problem solving.

### 27-30
Ryan: OK, then we need to explain how we measured whether the notes actually helped.
Emma: Well, we both tested ourselves on the content of the lecture without looking at our notes, and then compared our scores.
Ryan: How long did we wait? Was it a day afterwards?
Emma: We did a quick test after twenty-four hours, but we decided that was too soon to show any real difference. We compared recall after forty-eight hours. That was when the difference really showed up. My score was quite a lot higher.
Ryan: But we have to be careful about how we present that. It was just the two of us, plus Sam. Our group was too small for strong conclusions.
Emma: Yes, and it was only one week of lectures. We should say that clearly at the start, so that nobody thinks we're claiming too much.
Ryan: Agreed. What about the future? Are you going to carry on with the Cornell method?
Emma: I think so, but with some changes. I'm going to stop trying to copy long quotations from the slides, because they're all available online anyway. And I plan to add example questions after class, the kind of questions that might come up in the exam, in the left-hand column. That way, the notes will be more useful for revision.
Ryan: That's a good idea. I might try that myself. Now, how are we going to show the other students the differences between the methods? Just describing them won't be very clear.
Emma: I thought about playing them the full recordings of the lecture, but that would take far too long. And handwritten posters wouldn't be easy to read from the back of the room.
Ryan: Why don't we just photograph our notes? We will explain differences with sample pages. One page from each method, side by side on the screen.
Emma: Perfect. Let's do that. I'll scan mine tonight.

## Part 4 | The Science of Sleep
intro: part of a lecture about sleep and its role in learning
voices: Lecturer=Charon

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. In this session of our series on the psychology of learning, I'm going to talk about sleep. Students often see sleep as something they can cut back on when they're busy, the night before a deadline, for instance. But as we'll see, there's growing evidence that sleep plays an important part in how we learn. I'll start with how the body regulates sleep, then look at its role in memory, and finish with some practical points about monitoring your own sleep.
Lecturer: Let's begin with timing. Most of us feel sleepy at roughly the same time each evening, and wake at roughly the same time each morning, even without an alarm clock. This isn't a coincidence. The body has an internal mechanism that controls when we feel alert and when we feel tired, as well as other processes such as the release of hormones. Researchers describe a daily timing system as the circadian clock. The word circadian comes from Latin, and means about a day.
Lecturer: Now, this internal clock isn't perfectly accurate. Left to itself, it tends to run slightly longer than twenty-four hours, so it has to be reset every day by signals from the environment. Food and exercise have some effect, but by far the most important factor is light. Daylight provides a strong time signal for the body, particularly in the morning. That's why people who spend their mornings indoors in dim light often find it harder to fall asleep at night.
Lecturer: Our habits matter too. Many students have very different routines during the week and at the weekend, getting up at seven on weekdays and at eleven on Saturdays, for example. This is sometimes called social jet lag, because the effect on the body is similar to flying across several time zones. Stable wake times can support a regular routine, and interestingly, the time you get up seems to matter more than the time you go to bed.
Lecturer: So why does any of this matter for learning? Well, for a long time, scientists believed that sleep was simply a period of rest for the brain. We now know that the brain is extremely active during sleep. Sleep plays a role in memory consolidation, the process by which new information is stabilised and linked to what we already know. In several experiments, people who slept after learning a list of words remembered more of them the next day than people who stayed awake for the same length of time.
Lecturer: A number of things can interfere with sleep, and I'll mention two of them. The first is caffeine, which is found in coffee, tea, many soft drinks and chocolate. Caffeine stays in the body for several hours, but its effect varies a great deal between individuals. Some people can drink coffee after dinner and sleep perfectly well, while others can't. Late caffeine may affect some people strongly, so it's worth noticing how you respond to it.
Lecturer: The second factor is light in the evening. As I mentioned, light tells the body that it's daytime. Bright screens and room lighting late at night can therefore delay the body's signal to sleep. Reducing light exposure at night helps create a quieter setting for sleep, for example by dimming the lights and putting your phone away for the last half hour before bed.
Lecturer: I should stress that there's no single correct bedtime. Individuals vary in their natural sleep timing. Some people are naturally early risers, so-called larks, while others, the owls, feel most alert late in the evening. These differences are partly genetic, and they also change with age. Teenagers and young adults tend to be later than older people.
Lecturer: If you want to understand your own sleep, one simple tool is a diary. A sleep diary records both bedtime and wake-up time, and many people also add notes about how rested they feel, or whether they had any caffeine. Keeping one for two weeks gives you a far more accurate picture than relying on memory.
Lecturer: When you look at your diary, try not to worry about individual nights. Everybody sleeps badly sometimes, before an exam, for example, or after a long journey. A single short night does not establish a chronic sleep problem. It's the pattern over several weeks that matters.
Lecturer: Finally, a word of caution. This lecture is about everyday sleep habits, not about medical conditions. Persistent concerns should be discussed with a health professional, such as your doctor or the university health centre, rather than trying to solve them on your own. In next week's session, we'll look at the effects of exercise on concentration.
