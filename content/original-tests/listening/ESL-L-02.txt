# ESL-L-02 | Original Listening Test 02

## Part 1 | Kayak Course Enquiry
intro: a conversation between a student and an outdoor centre employee about a kayaking session
voices: Agent=Charon, Student=Zephyr

### 1-6
Agent: Good afternoon, Seal Bay Outdoor Centre. How can I help you?
Student: Hi. I'm calling about your kayaking courses. I saw a poster at my college, and I'd like to book a session for myself, and possibly a friend. I've never been kayaking before, though.
Agent: That's no problem at all. Most of the people who come to us are complete beginners. We run a beginner session in Sheltered Cove, which is perfect if you've never been in a kayak. Can I ask when you were hoping to come?
Student: Some time in May, ideally. I've got exams at the beginning of the month, so it would need to be after that.
Agent: Let me check. The sessions on the fifth are already full, I'm afraid. The beginner session is available on 12 May. There are still six places left on that one.
Student: The twelfth would be perfect. That's a Saturday, isn't it?
Agent: That's right. The session itself starts at nine, but we always give a short talk beforehand, so please arrive by 8:40 a.m. for the safety briefing. Anyone who misses the briefing can't go on the water, so it's important to be on time.
Student: OK, eight forty. And where exactly do we go? Is it the centre on the main road?
Agent: No, that's just our office. We don't launch from there. We meet outside the lifeboat station. It's at the far end of the harbour, past the fish market, and there's a car park right behind it.
Student: Great. Could you tell me how much it costs? The poster said something about a discount for students.
Agent: There's a student discount on the full-day course, but not on the beginner session, I'm afraid. The two-hour session costs 42 pounds. That includes all the equipment and the instructor.
Student: That's fine. Now, my friend's younger brother might want to come too. He's thirteen. Would that be OK?
Agent: I'm sorry, but he wouldn't be able to join this one. Participants must be at least 14 years old. We do run family sessions in the summer holidays, though, where younger children can paddle with a parent.
Student: OK, I'll let him know. And what should we wear? I don't have any special clothing.
Agent: You don't need anything special. Wear clothes you don't mind getting wet, something light, like a T-shirt and shorts or leggings. It can get quite cold out on the water, even in May. Wear a windproof jacket over your other clothes. A cotton hoodie isn't a good idea, because it gets very heavy when it's wet.
Student: I've got a running jacket that should work.
Agent: That sounds ideal.

### 7-10
Student: Do we need to bring anything else? I wasn't sure whether we should buy any equipment.
Agent: No, please don't buy anything. Some people turn up with waterproof hats or rubber boots, but honestly, they're not much use in a kayak. We supply a buoyancy vest with every kayak, and the instructor will check that it fits properly before you go out.
Student: That's reassuring. What happens if the weather's bad? I've heard the bay can get quite rough.
Agent: It can. A bit of rain doesn't matter, because you're going to get wet anyway, so we don't worry about rainfall. And the session finishes long before sunset, of course. The final decision depends on the wind forecast. We check it early on the morning of the session.
Student: And if it's too windy, is the session cancelled?
Agent: Usually not. We have a backup plan. If the sea is rough, we switch to the lake. It's about fifteen minutes inland, and we take everyone there in our minibus.
Student: Oh, that's good. I was worried we'd end up in an indoor pool.
Agent: No, no. Some centres do that, but we prefer to stay outdoors. We did once try using the river, but the current was too strong for beginners, so we don't go there any more.
Student: Fair enough. So how do I book?
Agent: I can book you now, over the phone. I'll just need your name and a contact number. Once you've paid, your confirmation will arrive by email. It includes a map and a list of what to bring.
Student: Not by text?
Agent: We only send a text if the location changes on the day. And we stopped sending anything by post a few years ago.
Student: OK. My name's Sophie Carter. That's C-A-R-T-E-R.
Agent: Lovely. Let's get you booked in, Sophie.

## Part 2 | Market Waste Tour
intro: a volunteer coordinator explaining how a city market reduces its waste
voices: Coordinator=Aoede

### 11-16
Coordinator: Hello, everyone, and thank you for coming along this morning. My name is Rosa Martinez, and I coordinate the volunteers here at Hillgate Market. Some of you are new volunteers, and some of you are visitors who are interested in our Zero Waste project, so I'll start by explaining what we do, and then give you some practical information for today.
Coordinator: Hillgate is one of the oldest markets in the city. We have around ninety stalls selling fruit and vegetables, fish, bread, cheese and flowers, and on a busy Saturday we can have twelve thousand visitors. Five years ago, the market was sending almost all of its rubbish to landfill, around four tonnes every week. Today, more than two thirds of it is recycled or composted, and we're very proud of that.
Coordinator: Most of you will be joining the compost tour, which shows you where the waste goes. Please note that it doesn't start here at the main entrance. The compost tour starts at the east gate, next to the flower stalls. Our volunteers there will be wearing green aprons.
Coordinator: So what kind of waste do we actually produce? People often assume it's mostly packaging, or waste from the fish stalls. In fact, most of our organic waste is vegetable scraps. Outer leaves, peelings, damaged produce that can't be sold. That's good news, because it's ideal material for composting.
Coordinator: Of course, it's only useful if it's separated properly. When we started the project, everything went into the same skip. Now stallholders use colour-coded bins. Green is for food waste, blue for cardboard and paper, and grey for anything that can't be recycled. Each stall has its own set.
Coordinator: Timing is important too. Food waste can attract pests very quickly, particularly in summer, so we don't leave it lying around. Food leftovers are collected daily, usually at about four o'clock, after the stalls close. Cardboard is collected twice a week.
Coordinator: Not everything that's left over is waste, though. We work hard to make sure that good food doesn't get thrown away. For example, unsold bread goes to a local food charity, which distributes it to families and shelters the same evening. Some of the fruit stalls do the same with their produce.
Coordinator: On the tour, you'll also see the composting area itself. We send most of our compost to a large site outside the city, but we keep a small batch here so that people can see how the process works. Our demonstration batch matures for twelve weeks. During that time, volunteers turn it regularly to let air in, and they check the temperature every few days.

### 17-20
Coordinator: Now I'd like to talk about some of the challenges we still face, and some new things we've introduced this year.
Coordinator: The biggest problem for composting is contamination, in other words, material in the food bins that shouldn't be there. You might expect broken glass jars, or pieces of the wooden crates that fruit is delivered in, but those are actually quite rare. The main contamination comes from plastic labels. Those little stickers on apples and bananas don't break down, and they end up in the finished compost.
Coordinator: Something new this year is our education programme. We've realised that many visitors want to know what they can do at home. So, visitors can join a soil workshop in the afternoon. It's run by a local gardener, and it covers how to make compost in a small garden, or even on a balcony. It's free, but places are limited, so please sign up at lunchtime.
Coordinator: We've also replaced all the signs above the bins. The old ones had far too much writing on them, and nobody read them. This time we did things differently. New signs were tested with stallholders before printing. We asked them which designs were clearest, and we chose the one with simple pictures and very few words.
Coordinator: Finally, if you'd like more information about any of this, we've produced a guide to the project. The old version is still on the noticeboard by the exit gate, but please ignore it, as some of the collection times have changed. And the main office is closed at weekends. The updated guide is available at the help desk, which is in the centre of the market, next to the clock.
Coordinator: Right. If you're joining the tour, please make your way to the east gate now. Thank you.

## Part 3 | Building Cooling Project
intro: two students, Priya and Jack, planning a project on low-energy ways of cooling buildings
voices: Priya=Leda, Jack=Puck

### 21-26
Priya: Hi Jack. Have you got a few minutes to go over our cooling project? I'd like to finalise the plan before we meet Dr Evans on Thursday.
Jack: Sure. I've been reading some of the background papers, actually, and I'm a bit worried that our aim is too broad.
Priya: Well, let's start there, then. In the proposal we said we were comparing low-energy ways of keeping rooms cool. But most of the studies I've read only look at how much money people save.
Jack: Yes, and that's not really what we're interested in. Our project focuses on indoor comfort, not just energy bills. Whether people can actually work in the rooms without feeling too hot.
Priya: Exactly. Now, where are we going to take the measurements? I asked about the library, but they said no, because of the students working there.
Jack: I spoke to the facilities manager last week. We can measure rooms in the engineering block. There are several seminar rooms on the second floor that are hardly used in the afternoons, and they're all the same size.
Priya: That's great. What about equipment? We've got the temperature sensors from the lab, but we also wanted to measure how much sunlight comes into the rooms.
Jack: That's sorted, too. The department will lend us a light meter. We just have to sign it out from the technician each day.
Priya: Brilliant. So, the actual comparison. We originally talked about testing fans, blinds and different types of glass.
Jack: I think that's too much for a short project. I'd suggest we keep it simple. We should compare windows with external shading, you know, those metal louvres on the outside of the building, and windows with ordinary blinds on the inside.
Priya: Yes, that's more manageable. Oh, and I forgot to tell you. I had a quick chat with our tutor yesterday. Our tutor says to record humidity as well as temperature. Apparently, a room can feel much hotter than the thermometer suggests if the air is damp.
Jack: That makes sense. The sensors we've got can do both, so that's easy. How long should we run the measurements for? I was thinking two weeks.
Priya: I don't think that's enough. One cloudy week could ruin everything. The measurements should run for four weeks. That still gives us time to analyse the data before the deadline.
Jack: Fine. Four weeks it is.

### 27-30
Jack: Now, there are a few things we need to be careful about in the analysis.
Priya: Yes. The first is timing. When do you think we'll see the biggest differences between the rooms?
Jack: Well, in the mornings the sun's on the other side of the building, so the rooms are fairly similar. We wondered about weekend mornings, but the heating system's switched off then, which would complicate things. And in the evenings the sun's too low to matter. Afternoons will give us the most glare. So that's when the shading should make the biggest difference.
Priya: Right. The second issue is the rooms themselves. They're the same size, the ceiling height is the same, and they've all been painted the same colour recently. But they don't all face the same way.
Jack: Good point. The rooms differ in orientation, so comparisons need care. Two of them face south-west and one faces west. We'll need to mention that in the limitations.
Priya: Definitely. Then there's how we present the results. I was going to use line graphs, showing the temperature through the day.
Jack: We'll need a few of those, but they won't show the relationship we're really interested in. We can show the results using scatter plots, with the amount of sunlight against the temperature for each room. Pie charts won't be any use at all.
Priya: Agreed. And finally, the recommendation. If external shading turns out to be better, we should say so, but I think we need to be realistic.
Jack: What do you mean?
Priya: Well, external louvres are expensive to install, but that's not the only cost. They need to be cleaned and repaired, especially after storms. The recommendation should address maintenance costs as well.
Jack: That's true. The university will definitely care about the long-term costs.
Priya: OK. I'll write that up and send it to you before Thursday.

## Part 4 | Mangrove Nurseries
intro: part of a lecture about restoring coastal mangrove forests
voices: Lecturer=Orus

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning. Today's lecture is about mangrove forests, and in particular about the work being done around the world to restore them. Mangroves are trees and shrubs that grow along tropical and subtropical coastlines, in the zone between the land and the sea. They're found in more than a hundred countries, but over the past fifty years a large proportion of them has been lost, mainly to fish farms, agriculture and coastal development. Restoring them has become a priority for many governments, and today I'll explain why, and look at what makes a restoration project succeed or fail.
Lecturer: Let's begin with what mangroves actually do. If you've ever seen a mangrove forest, you'll know that the trees have extraordinary root systems, tangled networks of roots that rise above the mud. These roots are not just for support. As the tide flows in and out, the water slows down as it passes through them. Mangrove roots slow the movement of sediment, so the mud and sand carried by the water settle among the trees rather than being washed away. Over time, this actually builds up the land and protects the coast from erosion and storms.
Lecturer: So if mangroves are so valuable, why can't we simply plant more of them? Well, many early restoration projects did exactly that. They planted millions of seedlings along the shore, and in many cases almost all of them died. The main reason was that they were planted in the wrong place. Mangroves can survive being covered by seawater for part of the day, but not all of the day. Young plants need suitable tidal levels. If they're planted too low on the shore, they're under water for too long. Too high, and they dry out.
Lecturer: Most successful projects now follow a two-stage process. Seeds are collected from healthy adult trees, but they aren't planted directly on the coast. Seedlings first grow in a sheltered nursery, where they're protected from the waves and can be watered and checked regularly. After several months, when they're strong enough, they're moved out to the planting sites.
Lecturer: Even then, the young trees face a number of threats. Some are natural. Crabs, for example, eat the seedlings. But one of the most serious problems is caused by people. Floating rubbish can damage young trees. Plastic bags and old fishing nets get caught around the stems and either break them or pull them over, so that they're buried in the mud. Many projects now organise regular clean-ups for this reason.
Lecturer: How do we know whether a project is working? In the past, organisations often reported the number of trees they had planted, which sounds impressive but tells us very little. Researchers measure the survival rate after planting, that is, the proportion of seedlings that are still alive after six months, a year, two years and so on. That gives a much more honest picture.
Lecturer: Another factor that has a big impact on survival is the choice of species. There are around seventy species of mangrove, and they're not all suited to the same conditions. Different species tolerate different levels of salt. Some grow best near the open sea, while others prefer areas where rivers bring in fresh water. Planting a single species along an entire coastline is therefore rarely a good idea.
Lecturer: I want to turn now to the role of local communities, because this is something the most successful projects have in common. Scientists may understand the biology of mangroves, but they often know very little about a particular stretch of coast. Local fishers provide detailed knowledge of channels: where the water flows, which areas flood during storms, and where mangroves used to grow before they were cleared.
Lecturer: Communities also influence the timing of the work. Planting is often organised around the rainy season, because the extra fresh water reduces stress on the young trees. But it also has to fit around people's lives, so projects avoid the busiest fishing periods, when volunteers wouldn't be available.
Lecturer: Visitors can be a problem too. Some restoration sites have become popular with tourists and school groups, which is good for raising awareness. But people walking across the mud can crush seedlings and compact the soil. Temporary paths prevent damage from foot traffic. These are usually simple wooden boardwalks that can be removed once the forest is established.
Lecturer: To conclude, then. Restoring mangroves is possible, but it's a slow process, and patience is essential. A project that looks successful after a few weeks may fail completely after the first big storm. That's why success is assessed over several years, not days. In the next lecture, we'll look at a case study from Southeast Asia, where a community project has been running for over twenty years.
