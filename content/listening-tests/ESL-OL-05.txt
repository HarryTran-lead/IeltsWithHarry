Ranger: Good evening, everyone, and thanks for coming to this residents' meeting. I'm Ana, the senior ranger at Eastmere River Park. I'd like to tell you about some of the work we've been doing over the last year, and about our plans for the summer.
Ranger: For those of you who are new to the area, a bit of background. The park was opened twelve years ago, on land beside the river that used to be fields. People often think it was created mainly for wildlife, and it's certainly become very important for birds. But the main reason the park was created was to hold back floodwater. The ponds and wet meadows store water during heavy rain, which protects the houses in the lower part of the town.
Ranger: Now, I have to talk about some problems. We've had complaints about cyclists and dogs, and we're dealing with those. But the biggest problem has been fires. Last summer there were six fires started by people having barbecues, and one of them destroyed a large area of reeds. So barbecues are now completely banned in the park.
Ranger: On a more positive note, many of you will have used the new boardwalk across the marsh. It's open all year round, and it means people can now walk right round the lake without getting their feet wet. We'd applied for money from local businesses, but in the end we didn't need it, because the boardwalk was built entirely by volunteers, using wood from trees that had fallen in the park.
Ranger: The boardwalk has made a real difference to the number of people who visit in winter. Before, the marsh was so muddy between November and March that hardly anyone walked round the lake. This January, our counters recorded more than three thousand people using it, which is more than double the figure for the same month last year.
Ranger: Some people have asked what to do if they find an injured animal. Please don't try to move it yourselves, and don't bring it to the ranger station, as we don't have facilities there. We don't have an app yet, although we're working on one. For now, please ring the number on any of the information boards, and someone will come out.
Ranger: Finally, you may be asked to fill in a short survey over the next few months. We're thinking about adding benches and more activities, but the survey has a different purpose. We're applying for funding for a new bus stop, so we need to know how people get here, whether they walk, cycle, drive or come by bus.

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. Today I want to introduce you to an animal that most people have never seen, even though it may be living in a river near you. I'm talking about the freshwater pearl mussel, and more generally about freshwater mussels, which are among the most endangered animals in the world. I'll start with some basic biology, then explain why they matter, why they've declined, and what's being done to save them.
Lecturer: Freshwater mussels are remarkable for their age. Some species can live for more than a century, which makes them among the longest-living animals on the planet. Scientists can work out their age by counting growth rings on their shells, rather like the rings in a tree.
Lecturer: They live half buried in the riverbed and feed by drawing water into their bodies and removing tiny particles, such as algae and bacteria. They then pump the clean water back out.
Lecturer: Their life cycle is quite extraordinary. The young mussels, which are microscopic, can't survive on their own at first. Instead, they attach themselves to the gills of fish, usually salmon or trout, and live there for several months, without harming the fish. Then they drop off and settle on the riverbed.
Lecturer: So why do mussels matter? The first reason is that they're extremely effective at cleaning water. Each adult can filter up to fifty litres a day, so a healthy population can clean a whole river. Where mussels have disappeared, the water often becomes cloudier.
Lecturer: They're also an important part of the food chain. Animals such as otters break open the shells to eat them, as do some birds. And even after the mussels die, they're still useful. Their shells create shelter for insects and small fish on the riverbed.
Lecturer: There's one more reason they matter to scientists. Because mussels stay in the same place for most of their long lives, and because they're very sensitive to pollution, they act as a kind of early warning system. If mussels in a river start to die, it's often the first sign that something is wrong with the water, long before the effects are visible in fish or plants.
Lecturer: Sadly, freshwater mussels have declined dramatically. In Europe, it's estimated that over ninety per cent of the freshwater pearl mussel population has disappeared in the last hundred years. In the past, the main cause was human collection. The freshwater pearl mussel was collected for its pearls for centuries, and in some rivers almost every mussel was taken. Today collecting them is illegal in most countries.
Lecturer: However, the biggest threat now is sediment. When forests are cut down or fields are ploughed near rivers, soil washes into the water. The sediment fills the gravel on the riverbed where the young mussels live, and they suffocate. Many rivers now contain only old mussels and no young ones at all.
Lecturer: Other human activities have also played a part. When rivers are straightened to reduce flooding, or when dams are built, the flow of water changes, and the gravel beds where mussels live can be washed away or buried. In some rivers, populations that had survived for thousands of years disappeared within a few decades of this kind of engineering.
Lecturer: Climate change is adding to the pressure. Rivers are becoming warmer in summer, and the fish that the young mussels depend on, especially salmon, are declining too. If there are no fish, the life cycle simply can't be completed, however clean the water is.
Lecturer: Fortunately, conservation projects are making progress. In several countries, young mussels are raised in tanks in special centres, where they're protected until they're large enough to survive. They're then released into carefully chosen rivers where the water quality has improved. Farmers are often involved too. In some areas they're paid to plant trees along riverbanks and to keep cattle away from the water, which reduces the amount of soil that washes in.
Lecturer: The young mussels are released in small numbers at first, sometimes in cages placed on the riverbed, so that scientists can check on them regularly. If they grow well, larger numbers are released in the following years.
Lecturer: But releasing mussels isn't the end of the story. Because they live so long, a river can appear to have a healthy population even if it's slowly dying. So scientists measure success by checking whether mussels breed in the wild, that is, whether young mussels are appearing naturally. In the next lecture, we'll look at a project in Scotland where that's finally happening.
