### 11-14
Officer: Good afternoon, everyone, and thank you for coming to Stonebridge Science Hub. I'm Kate, the education officer here, and I'm going to give you an overview of what we offer schools, so that you can plan your visits.
Officer: First of all, as some of you will know, we moved into this new building in the spring. The old building was in the town centre, and it was very cramped. This one was designed to be as environmentally friendly as possible. We have solar panels, although they only provide about half of our electricity, and there's a garden on the roof, where children can see how plants help to keep a building cool.
Officer: People often ask whether the new building was expensive. It certainly wasn't cheap. It actually cost a good deal more than we'd planned, but we were lucky to get support from local businesses. The biggest difference for schools is space. In the old building we could only manage one class at a time, but here we can now welcome two school groups at the same time, without them getting in each other's way.
Officer: Now, let me tell you about the activities. They're all hands-on, but some are better suited to older pupils than others. The model bridge challenge, for example, involves quite a lot of measuring, so it's best for children aged nine and above. The same is true of the weather station, where pupils take readings and enter them on a computer.
Officer: For younger children, I'd particularly recommend two things. The light and colour room is ideal for the youngest pupils, because they can play with prisms and coloured shadows, and there's very little reading involved. And the fossil dig is another favourite with five- and six-year-olds. They dig in a big sandpit and find copies of real fossils, which they can then identify using picture cards.
Officer: The chemistry demonstration is very popular too, but we only run it for secondary pupils, for safety reasons.
Officer: Whatever activities you choose, I'd suggest planning no more than three in a day. Teachers sometimes try to fit in everything, and the children end up rushing from room to room. Most of our activities last about forty minutes, and pupils get much more out of them if they have time to ask questions at the end.

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning. Today I'm going to talk about one of the most remarkable things in the natural world: bird migration. Every year, billions of birds travel thousands of kilometres between their breeding grounds and the places where they spend the winter, and many of them return to exactly the same spot, year after year. I'll look at why birds migrate, how they prepare, how they find their way, and finally at some of the threats they face.
Lecturer: So, why do birds undertake these long and dangerous journeys? The main reason is that the seasons change the resources available. In the northern summer, there are long days and an enormous number of insects, but in winter that disappears. So birds move to where they can find a reliable supply of food throughout the year.
Lecturer: But that doesn't explain why they come back. Why not just stay in the tropics? One important reason is that in the north there's less competition from other species for nesting sites and food, so birds can raise more young there during the summer.
Lecturer: Not all birds migrate, of course. Some species stay in the same area all year round, and others only move short distances, perhaps from the mountains down to the valleys in winter. Scientists call these partial migrants, and they're very useful for research, because individuals of the same species that behave differently can be compared.
Lecturer: Preparing for migration is a huge physical challenge. Before setting off, many birds eat almost constantly for several weeks. They store fat, which provides the energy for the journey, and some small songbirds actually double their body weight. Their bodies change in other ways too. Some birds even reduce the size of their digestive organs, so they're lighter during the flight.
Lecturer: How do birds know when it's time to leave? Temperature plays a part, but the main trigger seems to be changes in day length. Experiments have shown that birds kept indoors become restless at the right time of year if the hours of light are changed, even when the temperature stays the same.
Lecturer: The distances involved can be astonishing. The Arctic tern, for example, flies from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back every year, a round trip of around seventy thousand kilometres. Over a lifetime of thirty years, that adds up to the equivalent of travelling to the moon and back three times.
Lecturer: Now, to the most fascinating question: how do they find their way? For many species, it seems that young birds inherit a sense of direction. In one famous experiment, young birds raised in isolation still set off in the correct direction, even though they'd never seen an adult make the journey.
Lecturer: But inherited direction isn't enough on its own. Birds also use several kinds of information from their surroundings. During the day, they use the position of the sun, adjusting for the time of day using their internal clock. At night, things are different. Some species have been shown to use patterns of stars, and in laboratory experiments with an artificial sky, birds changed direction when the stars were moved.
Lecturer: Perhaps most extraordinary of all, birds can detect the Earth's magnetic field. Scientists believe that special cells in their eyes allow them to sense it, which means they may actually be able to see it in some way. This is particularly useful when the sky is cloudy.
Lecturer: And there's growing evidence that smell is important too, especially for seabirds. Species such as petrels fly across huge stretches of open ocean, and it seems they use smell to locate the islands where they breed, possibly by recognising the scent of particular plants or chemicals in the air.
Lecturer: What's remarkable is that birds seem to combine all these sources of information, and switch between them when one isn't available. If the sun is hidden, they rely more on the magnetic sense, for example. Researchers are still working out exactly how the brain puts all of this together.
Lecturer: Finally, let me turn to the threats. Climate change is altering the timing of insect populations, so birds may arrive too late to find food for their young. But a more immediate problem in cities is artificial lighting. Birds migrating at night can become confused by brightly lit buildings, and many are killed in collisions. Some cities now ask office buildings to switch off their lights during the main migration season. In the next lecture, we'll look at how successful those schemes have been.
