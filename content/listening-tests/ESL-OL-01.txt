### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning, everyone. Today I'm going to talk about a phenomenon that affects almost every large city in the world, and which is becoming more important as global temperatures rise. It's known as the urban heat island effect. Put simply, cities tend to be warmer than the countryside around them, sometimes by as much as five or six degrees. I'll look first at the causes, then at the effects on people and the environment, and finally at some of the solutions that cities are trying.
Lecturer: So, why are cities warmer? The first reason is the materials we build with. Roads, pavements and roofs are often dark, and dark surfaces absorb a great deal of energy from the sun during the day. Then they release it slowly at night, which is why the difference between cities and the countryside is actually greatest after dark, not in the middle of the afternoon.
Lecturer: The shape of the city matters too. In the countryside, wind moves freely across open fields. But in a city centre, tall buildings reduce the flow of air through the streets, so the warm air simply stays where it is. Engineers sometimes describe these streets as canyons.
Lecturer: The third cause is heat that we produce ourselves. Every car engine gives off heat, and so do factories. But one source that people often forget is cooling equipment. The units that cool our offices and shops work by pumping hot air outside, straight into the street. So the more we try to cool our buildings, the hotter the city becomes.
Lecturer: Let's move on to the effects. The most obvious one is on energy use. During a heatwave, demand for electricity rises sharply, mainly because of air-conditioning, and in some cities this has led to power cuts.
Lecturer: The effect on health is also serious. You might expect the main danger to come from the heat during the day, but in fact many doctors are more concerned about hot nights. When the temperature stays high at night, it disturbs people's sleep, and the body never gets a chance to recover. This is particularly dangerous for older residents, who may also live alone.
Lecturer: There are environmental effects as well. When it rains after a hot day, the water running off roads and car parks is much warmer than normal. It flows into drains and then, of course, into streams, and this sudden change in temperature can kill the fish in local rivers.
Lecturer: So what can be done? One of the simplest solutions is to change the colour of roofs. A roof painted white can reflect as much as eighty per cent of the sunlight that falls on it, compared with perhaps twenty per cent for a dark roof. Some cities now require new buildings to have light-coloured roofs.
Lecturer: Another option is the green roof, where plants are grown on top of a building. These are very effective at cooling, and they also absorb rainwater. However, they aren't a solution you can simply install and forget. They need regular maintenance, including watering in dry weather, and without it the plants quickly die.
Lecturer: Trees are probably the most popular solution, and they do a great deal more than provide shade. But where you plant them matters. Research in several cities has found that trees are most effective along streets that run from east to west, because those are the streets that receive direct sun for most of the day.
Lecturer: Finally, a point about planning. Heat doesn't affect all parts of a city equally. Some neighbourhoods, often the poorer ones with fewer parks, can be several degrees hotter than others just a few kilometres away. To target their spending properly, planners need much better data about where the hottest areas actually are, and some cities are now using sensors on buses to collect it. In the next lecture, we'll look at one of those cities in detail.
