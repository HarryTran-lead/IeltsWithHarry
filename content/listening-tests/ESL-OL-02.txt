### 11-14
Manager: Good morning, and welcome to Fernbank Wildlife Centre. I'm Hemi, the centre manager, and I'm delighted that so many of you have signed up as volunteers this season. I'll start with a little background and some ground rules, and then I'll tell you about the different parts of the site.
Manager: Fernbank isn't a zoo, and it was never meant to be one. Thirty years ago, it was started by a local vet to look after animals hit by cars on the busy roads around here, mostly hedgehogs, badgers and owls. These days we do some work with birds whose nesting areas have been damaged by building, but treatment of injured animals is still the heart of what we do, and we release almost all of them once they've recovered.
Manager: There's some good news to share. As you may know, we've struggled with money for a few years, and at one point we thought we might have to close on weekdays. But we've just received a grant from a national wildlife charity, which means we can keep our normal hours, and school visits will continue to be free.
Manager: The grant also means we can finally replace the roof of the hospital building, which has been leaking for years, and buy a second van, so that we can collect injured animals more quickly. We hope all of that will be finished before the winter, when we usually have the most patients.
Manager: Now, a word about the animals themselves. Many of you will be helping with feeding, and you'll be shown exactly what to do on your first day, so please don't worry about that. Some of the animals are in enclosures that visitors can see, and others are recovering in the hospital building. Wherever they are, they can easily become stressed by noise. So please keep your voices down when you're in the animal areas, and remind visitors to do the same.
Manager: Finally, a practical point. Some of you will be cycling here, and there's a bike rack by the gate. If you're coming by car, you can park in our own car park at the end of the track. But please drive very slowly along the track, because hedgehogs and even young deer often cross it, especially in the early morning.

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good afternoon. Today's lecture is about an ecosystem that very few people have heard of, even though it's found along the coasts of every continent except Antarctica. I'm talking about seagrass meadows. I'll explain what seagrass is, why it matters, what's threatening it, and what's being done to bring it back.
Lecturer: Let's start with a common misunderstanding. Because it grows under the sea and looks rather like long green ribbons, people often assume seagrass is a kind of algae. But it isn't a type of seaweed at all. It's a flowering plant, more closely related to grasses and lilies, and it produces flowers and seeds under the water.
Lecturer: Like all plants, seagrass needs sunlight to grow, so you'll only find it in fairly shallow coastal waters, where there's enough light reaching the sea floor. If the water becomes cloudy, the meadows quickly begin to die.
Lecturer: So why should we care about seagrass? Its first major value is for marine life. The dense leaves provide shelter from predators, and in many areas seagrass acts as a nursery for young fish, including several species that are important to commercial fishing.
Lecturer: Many of these fish travel long distances once they're adult, which means that the loss of a meadow in one bay can affect fishing catches many kilometres away.
Lecturer: Seagrass also protects the coastline itself. Its roots spread out horizontally and hold sediment together, and the leaves slow down the movement of waves, so the meadows help to reduce coastal erosion, especially during storms.
Lecturer: But the reason seagrass has attracted so much attention recently is carbon. Seagrass meadows take carbon from the water and store it in the sediment beneath them, where it can remain for thousands of years. Some studies suggest that seagrass captures carbon up to thirty-five times faster than tropical forests, even though the meadows cover a tiny fraction of the area.
Lecturer: There's one more benefit that's often overlooked. Seagrass meadows actually improve the quality of the water around them. The leaves trap tiny particles that would otherwise make the water cloudy, and some studies have found that the meadows reduce the number of harmful bacteria in the water, which is good news for both swimmers and coral reefs nearby.
Lecturer: Unfortunately, seagrass is disappearing. It's estimated that we've lost around a third of the world's meadows. One of the main causes is water pollution. In particular, fertiliser washed off farmland ends up in the sea, where it encourages the growth of algae. The algae blocks the light, and the seagrass dies.
Lecturer: Physical damage is the other major threat. In bays that are popular with tourists, the meadows are torn up by boat anchors every time a yacht stops for the night. If you fly over some of these bays, you can actually see the circular scars on the sea floor.
Lecturer: The good news is that seagrass can be restored, and there are some very successful projects. In one scheme in the UK, volunteers collect seeds from healthy meadows during the summer, by hand, while snorkelling. The seeds are then stored in tanks until the autumn.
Lecturer: Collecting seeds is slow work, and it can only be done for a few weeks each year, when the plants are flowering. But it has one big advantage over the older method, which involved digging up whole plants from one meadow and moving them to another. That approach damaged the healthy meadows, and many of the transplanted plants didn't survive the move.
Lecturer: Planting them is surprisingly simple. The seeds are mixed with sand and put into small hessian bags, which are dropped onto the sea floor from a boat. The bags are made from natural fibres, so they gradually rot away as the young plants become established.
Lecturer: Monitoring is also part of the work. Divers return to the planted areas every few months to count the young plants and measure how far the meadow has spread. In the first year, survival can be quite low, especially after storms, but the plants that do survive spread surprisingly quickly once their roots are established.
Lecturer: Researchers have found, however, that technical methods are only part of the story. Projects only succeed in the long term if they have the support of local communities, particularly fishers and boat owners, who need to agree to keep away from the planted areas. Where that cooperation exists, the meadows recover remarkably quickly. Next time, we'll look at a project in Wales where exactly that has happened.
