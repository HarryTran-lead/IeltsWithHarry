### 1-5
Assistant: Good afternoon, Clayworks. How can I help?
Helen: Hello. I'm interested in your pottery classes. I've never done any pottery before, so I'm looking for something for complete beginners.
Assistant: Then you've come to the right place. We run four different beginners' classes at the moment. Have you seen the list on our website?
Helen: I had a quick look, but it wasn't very clear. Could you go through them for me?
Assistant: Of course. The most popular one is Wheel Basics, on Monday evenings. That's where you learn to use the potter's wheel. It's twenty-five pounds a session, and there's a maximum of six people, because we only have six wheels.
Helen: I'm not sure I'm ready for the wheel. Is there anything a bit simpler?
Assistant: Yes, a lot of people prefer to start with hand-building, where you shape the clay with your hands and simple tools. It used to run on Wednesdays, but the hand-building class is on Tuesday evenings now, from seven till nine. It's eighteen pounds.
Helen: Tuesday would suit me better, actually. What do you make in that class?
Assistant: In the first session, everyone makes a small bowl. In later sessions people go on to make plates and vases, but the bowl is a good way to learn the basic techniques.
Helen: That sounds lovely. And what about glazing? Is that a separate class?
Assistant: It is. Glazing is when you add colour and a shiny surface to your pieces once they've been fired in the kiln. That class is on Thursdays. It was twenty pounds last year, but the price of materials has gone up, so that one's twenty-two pounds.
Helen: OK. Do I need to bring anything?
Assistant: For the glazing class, the glazes can splash quite a lot, so bring an old shirt to wear over your clothes. We provide aprons in the other classes, but they don't really cover your arms.
Helen: Right. And I noticed something about a family class. My nephew might enjoy that.
Assistant: Yes, Family Clay is on Saturday mornings. It's thirty pounds for one adult and one child. We used to take children from five, but they found it a bit difficult, so now it's for children over seven.
Helen: He's nine, so that's fine.

### 6-10

Guide: So, if you start walking up Mill Lane towards Weaver Street, the first building on your left is the glass studio. You can usually see the furnaces glowing through the windows, so it's hard to miss.
Guide: If you're hungry, the bakery café is in the building next to the glass studio, further along Weaver Street towards the west. It does wonderful bread, and it's a good place to sit outside when it's sunny.
Guide: Now, if you keep going along Weaver Street, past the café, and cross over, then at the far western end of Weaver Street, on the canal side, is the sculpture yard. It's an open-air space where artists work on very large pieces in stone and metal, and you're welcome to wander in and watch. The building between the sculpture yard and Mill Lane is used for artists' studios, and they're private, I'm afraid.
Guide: Let's go back to Mill Lane. If you cross Weaver Street and continue north towards the canal, the community garden is the block on your right, between Mill Lane and the theatre. Local residents grow vegetables there, and there's a small pond.
Guide: Beyond the garden you'll come to the Old Mill Theatre, which is the large building at the eastern end of Weaver Street, backing onto the canal. It was the original mill, and it now has a programme of plays and concerts all year round.
Guide: Finally, back on the south side of Weaver Street, you'll see Market Square, which is to the east of Mill Lane. There are two buildings on the far side of the square. ### 17-20
Guide: Now, a little bit of history. Many people assume that this area was built as housing for factory workers, but in fact the buildings were warehouses, used to store wool that arrived by road from the hill farms. By the 1980s, they were empty and falling apart, and in the 1990s the council almost knocked the whole area down to build a car park. It was a group of local artists who persuaded them to change their minds, and they moved in soon afterwards.
Guide: A lot of visitors ask whether they can have a go at some of the crafts themselves. The answer is yes, but places are limited. If you'd like to try glassblowing, for example, you need to book a place at the visitor kiosk, behind me. There are sessions every day at eleven and at three. Please don't go straight to the studio, because the artists can't take bookings while they're working.
Guide: There are two markets in the quarter. The day market takes place in Market Square every Saturday, with stalls selling crafts and local produce. Then, in the summer, we have an evening market on Fridays. The stalls are much the same, and it closes at ten, but in the evening there's live music and food stalls from all over the world, so it has quite a different atmosphere.
Guide: And finally, a word of warning about the canal towpath. It's a lovely walk, and it's quite wide, so it's fine for pushchairs and bicycles. But the stones are very old and smooth, and the towpath can be very slippery just behind the theatre, especially after rain. So please take care there.
Guide: Right, that's all from me. Enjoy your afternoon, and do come back to the kiosk if you have any questions.

### 31-40
Lecturer: Good morning. In today's lecture, I'm going to look at the history of public clocks, the large clocks you see on towers, town halls and railway stations. We don't think about them much these days, because we all carry the time around on our phones. But for several centuries, these clocks shaped the way people organised their lives, and I'd argue they helped to create the modern idea of time itself.
Lecturer: Let's begin before there were any clocks at all. In medieval Europe, most people didn't need to know the exact time. They worked from sunrise to sunset. Where some kind of timekeeping was needed, for example for religious services or markets, towns relied on church bells, which were rung by hand at certain times of the day.
Lecturer: The first mechanical clocks appeared in the late thirteenth century. Interestingly, these early clocks had no hands at all. They didn't have a face that you could look at. Instead, they simply struck a bell automatically every hour, so they were really machines for making a noise rather than for displaying the time.
Lecturer: Over the next few centuries, public clocks spread across Europe, and they became much more than practical objects. Towns competed with each other to build the largest and most elaborate clocks, with moving figures and astronomical displays. A great clock was a symbol of the town's wealth, and a way of showing visitors how successful it was.
Lecturer: However, there was a problem that people today find quite surprising. Every town kept its own local time, based on the position of the sun. So when it was noon in London, it was a few minutes earlier in Bristol, further west. For most of history this didn't matter, because people rarely travelled far, and travel was slow.
Lecturer: It was the railways that changed everything. If every station kept a different time, it was impossible to run trains on a network. The railway companies needed a standard time, so that timetables could be printed that made sense to passengers everywhere. In Britain, railway time was adopted in the 1840s, and eventually it became the official time for the whole country.
Lecturer: This created a new demand for accuracy. Station clocks in particular had to agree with each other. So a system was introduced whereby a time signal was sent by telegraph every day from the Royal Observatory, and station masters adjusted their clocks to match it.
Lecturer: The clocks themselves also became more precise. The key development here was the pendulum. Large tower clocks used a long, heavy pendulum, sometimes several metres in length, which swung very regularly and wasn't easily affected by wind or vibration. Some of these clocks were accurate to within a second a week.
Lecturer: So what has happened to public clocks in more recent times? In the twentieth century, as cheap watches became common, many towns stopped looking after their clocks. A lot of mechanical tower clocks were replaced by electric ones, or simply allowed to stop, because the repair costs were so high, and very few clockmakers had the necessary skills.
Lecturer: Recently, though, there's been a revival of interest. Some cities have realised that historic clocks are part of what makes their streets attractive, and they now promote them as a way of attracting tourists, with guided tours of the clock towers.
Lecturer: And in many smaller towns, volunteers have formed groups to look after the clocks themselves. They wind them every week, carry out basic repairs and record their history, often using old photographs and documents. In the next lecture, we'll hear from one of these volunteers about the restoration of a clock built in 1784.
